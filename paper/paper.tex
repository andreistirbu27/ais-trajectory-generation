\begin{abstract}
Predicting the path a vessel will take between two known points is a
central problem in maritime routing, logistics, and anomaly detection.
Given a start position, an end position, and a vessel type, we generate
a 128-waypoint route consistent with historical AIS traffic and the
geography of navigable water. Existing AIS forecasting methods, notably
the recent TrAISformer baseline, predict the next position from a
sliding-window history and have no native notion of long-range endpoint
conditioning. We propose a retrieval-augmented encoder--decoder
Transformer that (i) cross-attends to $K$ historical routes at every
decoder step, preserving per-timestep shape information that prior
mean-pool retrieval discards, (ii) is trained with a differentiable
signed-distance penalty over a coastline raster, biasing rollouts off
land, and (iii) at inference time projects on-land predictions onto a
fine-grained water-cell graph, making the output structurally
water-valid. On a cleaned $61\,K$-trajectory US-coastal corpus our full
system reaches $6.05\,\pm\,0.23$\,km ADE, a $31$\% reduction over a
great-circle baseline and a $48$\% reduction over zero-training
retrieval-top-1, with $0.0$\% land-crossing on every seed at a $10$\,km
clearance threshold. To enable an apples-to-apples comparison against
TrAISformer despite the two methods solving structurally different
tasks, we introduce a $K$-anchor benchmark that places $K$ ground-truth
positions along each trajectory and scores ADE on the non-anchor
indices. Sweeping $K \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 64\}$ on TrAISformer's native
Danish-AIS test set surfaces three distinct curve shapes ---
endpoint-conditioned ($K$-invariant low ADE), pure interpolation
(monotonic decay), and next-step prediction ($K$-invariant moderate
ADE) --- exposing which task each method actually solves rather than
collapsing the comparison to a single scalar. At the deployment-relevant
$K=2$ setting our model wins by $40$\% over TrAISformer's published
best-of-16 protocol. All code, configs, and the cleaned corpus are
released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Operational uses of Automatic Identification System (AIS) data ---
estimated time of arrival, port management, route-anomaly detection,
environmental impact assessment --- ask the same question: \emph{given
that a vessel is at point $A$ now and will be at point $B$ later, what
path will it take in between?} The endpoints are typically known at
query time (a freight contract, a dispatched destination, a port
schedule), and the downstream consumer wants the \emph{whole route}
that the vessel will traverse, not just its next ten minutes of motion.

The dominant strategy in the AIS literature has been the opposite one.
TrAISformer~\citep{traisformer2024} and GeoTrackNet~\citep{geotracknet2018}
discretise the operating region, train a Transformer or recurrent
auto-regressor to predict the next 10-minute AIS report given an 18-step
sliding-window history, and unroll for a few hours. Three pathologies
follow. First, autoregressive error compounds: published per-step ADE
of $\sim$1\,km at one hour balloons to tens of kilometres after a
twelve-hour rollout. Second, the predictor has no notion of \emph{land}:
its embedding lattice is uniform over the region's bounding box, and
nothing in the training loss penalises a predicted position that lies
inside a continent. Third, the conditioning is wrong for the operational
question --- the user has $(A, B)$, not the last three hours of
positions.

\paragraph{This paper.} We reframe the problem as
\emph{endpoint-conditioned route generation}. Given a start position
$A$, an end position $B$, and a vessel type $v$, we generate a complete
$T=128$-waypoint trajectory that (i) respects $(A, B)$, (ii) matches the
shape statistics of historical AIS traffic, and (iii) is structurally
water-valid (no land crossings) at a $10$\,km clearance threshold. Our
model is an encoder--decoder Transformer with two unusual ingredients.
First, the decoder cross-attends to $K$ historical analogues of the
queried $(A, B, v)$ tuple \emph{at every timestep} rather than to a
mean-pooled summary --- the route's \emph{shape} (not just its
endpoints) survives into the prediction. Second, training is regularised
by a differentiable signed-distance penalty against a coastline raster,
and inference snaps any residual on-land prediction onto a fine-grained
water-cell graph. The soft and hard constraints together close to
$0.00$\% land-crossings on every random seed without measurable ADE
cost.

\paragraph{The $K$-anchor protocol.}
A head-to-head comparison with TrAISformer is non-trivial because the
two methods solve structurally different tasks: TrAISformer outputs a
next-step prediction from a history; we output a full route from
endpoints. Reporting either's preferred metric in isolation is
incommensurable. We resolve this with a $K$-anchor benchmark
(\Cref{sec:k_anchor}) that places $K$ evenly-spaced ground-truth anchor
positions on each trajectory and scores ADE on the $T-K$ non-anchor
indices. Sweeping $K \in \{2,4,8,16,64\}$ generates an ADE-vs-$K$ curve
whose \emph{shape} characterises what task a method actually solves:
\emph{flat-low} for endpoint-conditioned methods, \emph{monotonic-down}
for pure interpolators, \emph{flat-moderate} for next-step predictors.
The protocol thereby surfaces the operating region of each method on a
single common axis (\Cref{fig:dma_k_anchor}).

\paragraph{Contributions.} We claim, in order of strength:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A retrieval-augmented endpoint-conditioned Transformer that
      reaches $6.05\,\pm\,0.23$\,km ADE on a $61$k-trajectory US-coastal
      corpus with $0$\% land-crossings on every seed --- a $31$\%
      reduction over a great-circle baseline and a $48$\% reduction
      over zero-training retrieval-top-1. The architectural delta over
      prior retrieval-augmented motion models is \emph{per-timestep}
      cross-attention to retrieved analogues; the mean-pool baseline
      ablation (\Cref{tab:ablation}) loses $1.2$\,km of ADE.
\item A symmetric soft / hard geographic constraint: a differentiable
      signed-distance penalty during training and an A*/snap projection
      onto a water-cell graph at inference. Either alone leaves $\sim$2\%
      land-crossings; together they reach $0$\%, and the
      constraint-consistent corpus filter ensures that the ADE penalty
      of enforcing water-validity is below seed variance.
\item A $K$-anchor unified benchmark on which next-step
      predictors and endpoint generators are directly comparable. On
      TrAISformer's native Danish-AIS dataset at the deployment-relevant
      $K{=}2$, our method beats TrAISformer's published best-of-16
      protocol by $40$\% in ADE.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Roadmap.} \Cref{sec:related} situates this work relative to
five lines of prior art (AIS prediction, Transformer trajectory
forecasting, sequence-to-sequence routing, geography-aware neural
networks, and retrieval-augmented generation). \Cref{sec:task} formalises
the endpoint-conditioned task, the $K$-anchor protocol, and the metric
suite. \Cref{sec:method} describes the architecture, the soft/hard

\Cref{sec:experiments} reports our results: headline numbers

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{AIS trajectory prediction.}
The dominant strand of work models AIS as a next-step prediction
problem on a discretised position-velocity grid.
GeoTrackNet~\citep{geotracknet2018} represents trajectories as
log-likelihood ratios over a $4$-D $(lat, lon, sog, cog)$ grid for
anomaly detection.  TrAISformer~\citep{traisformer2024} extends this to
a GPT-style transformer that auto-regresses the same discretised tokens,
reporting best-of-16 ADE of $\sim 1$\,km at the $1$-hour horizon on
Danish AIS.  Both methods require an 18-step sliding-window history,
neither model land, and neither targets long-horizon endpoint
conditioning.  Earlier work~\citep{capobianco2021, forti2020}
explores LSTM-based next-step prediction with similar history-driven
framing.  Our method differs on all three axes: we condition on
$(A, B)$ endpoints rather than a history window, score full routes
rather than a few hours of rollout, and enforce structural
water-validity as a first-class metric.

\paragraph{Transformer trajectory forecasting in other domains.}
Pedestrian, autonomous-driving, and sports-tracking literature
(Trajectron++~\citep{trajectronpp2020}, AgentFormer~\citep{agentformer2021},
HiVT~\citep{hivt2022}, MTR~\citep{mtr2022}) all share AIS work's
sliding-window framing: the model sees $H$ past positions of a target
agent (and optionally social context) and predicts the next $F$
positions. Anchor-based motion transformers
(MTR) introduce learned mode anchors that are conceptually adjacent to
our $K$-anchor protocol, but the anchors there are model-side priors
over goal locations rather than user-provided GT positions; the metric
question is also different (best-of-$k$ over candidate modes vs.\ ADE
on non-anchor indices). We borrow nothing technical from these works
beyond the decoder--cross-attention pattern, but their evaluation
framing motivates why the $K$-anchor curve is a more informative
diagnostic than a single ADE number.

\paragraph{Sequence-to-sequence and graph-based route prediction.}
Routing on transportation networks has been tackled with Pointer
Networks~\citep{pointernet2015}, Graph Attention Networks for
road-network shortest paths, and more recently neural CO solvers for
the TSP/VRP family. Our problem differs: the operating space is the
open ocean, not a road network, so there is no edge graph available
\emph{a priori}; we discover the navigable space at inference time
through retrieval and SDF queries. Diffusion-based route planners have
recently appeared in robotics~\citep{motiondiffuser2023}; our v13
diffusion variant (\Cref{sec:method:diffusion}) brings the same
ingredients to the AIS setting but with an EDM
preconditioner~\citep{edm2022} and trajectory-level anchor clamping.

\paragraph{Constrained, physics-informed, and geography-aware neural
networks.} Physics-informed networks~\citep{pinn2019} enforce
PDE constraints through soft loss terms; DeepSDF~\citep{deepsdf2019}
learns continuous signed-distance representations of object surfaces.
Our land penalty borrows the soft-constraint formulation of the
former and the SDF representation of the latter:
$L_{\text{land}} = \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{sdf}(\hat p) - \tau)^2]$,
differentiated through bilinear interpolation on a precomputed coastline
raster. Trajectron++~\citep{trajectronpp2020} similarly uses
dynamic-constraint penalties on predicted accelerations but does not
include any geographic-validity term. Safe-RL approaches enforce hard
constraints via Lagrangian methods; our hard constraint is enforced
post-hoc at inference rather than through training reward shaping,
which keeps the model architecture simple.

\paragraph{Retrieval-augmented generation.}
The recent retrieval-augmented language model literature
(RETRO~\citep{retro2022}, kNN-LM~\citep{knnlm2020},
RAG~\citep{rag2020}) demonstrates large quality gains from
in-context retrieval over neural-memory-only baselines on text. We
import the same idea for trajectories: a kNN index over
$(A, B, v)$ tuples returns $K=5$ analogues for the current query,
which the decoder cross-attends to throughout generation. The
architectural delta from prior trajectory work is the
\emph{per-timestep} cross-attention: rather than encode each retrieved
route into a single $d_{\text{model}}$-dim summary
(mean-pool, the v9 baseline), we tokenise each retrieved route into $t_{\text{retr}}$
position-aware tokens so the decoder can attend to ``neighbour $k$ at
phase $t$'' directly. \Cref{tab:ablation} shows this representation
choice is load-bearing on long routes.

\paragraph{Positioning.}
Our work is the first AIS-prediction system that (a) targets
endpoint-conditioned generation rather than next-step prediction;
(b) reports geographic validity as a first-class metric and achieves
$0$\% land-crossings on every seed; (c) compares against the
TrAISformer baseline on a shared protocol that does not privilege
either method's preferred input format. The technical contributions
--- per-timestep retrieval cross-attention, the symmetric soft/hard
geographic constraint, and the $K$-anchor benchmark --- are
individually small but combine into the first deployable AIS route
generator on the open-coast US setting.

\section{Task and Notation}
\label{sec:task}

\subsection{Endpoint-conditioned route generation}
Given a start position $\mathbf{p}_0 = (\lambda_0, \phi_0) \in \mathbb{R}^2$
(longitude, latitude), an end position $\mathbf{p}_{T-1} \in \mathbb{R}^2$,
and a vessel-type label $v \in \mathcal{V}$ (e.g.\ cargo, tanker, passenger,
with $|\mathcal{V}|=28$ in our corpus), the task is to produce
a sequence of $T=128$ waypoints
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{0:T-1} = (\hat{\mathbf{p}}_0, \hat{\mathbf{p}}_1, \dots, \hat{\mathbf{p}}_{T-1})
\in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 2}
\end{equation}
such that $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_0 = \mathbf{p}_0$, $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{T-1} = \mathbf{p}_{T-1}$
exactly, and the interior waypoints $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{1:T-2}$ minimise
the expected haversine deviation from a ground-truth trajectory
$\mathbf{p}_{0:T-1}^\star$ traversed by a real vessel of type $v$ between
those endpoints.

We adopt the \emph{arc-length-uniform} parameterisation: each ground-truth
trajectory is resampled (via linear interpolation on the cumulative
along-path distance) so that consecutive waypoints $\mathbf{p}_t^\star$ and
$\mathbf{p}_{t+1}^\star$ are equidistant in geodesic distance for all $t$.
This decouples the parameterisation from the original AIS sampling cadence
(which varies with vessel speed and AIS class) and from per-track wall-clock
duration. A 4-hour port maneuver and a 24-hour ocean crossing both become
$T=128$ equally-spaced points, and the metric ADE is comparable across
them. The price is that our predictions speak about \emph{spatial shape},
not about time: when a vessel will be at each waypoint is recoverable
from path length and an assumed speed but is not directly modelled. For
the operational tasks in \Cref{sec:intro} (ETA, anomaly detection, port
management) the spatial shape is the primary unknown; AIS sampling is
dense enough that timestamps along the predicted path can be reconstructed
post-hoc when needed.

\subsection{The $K$-anchor unified protocol}
\label{sec:k_anchor}

For each ground-truth trajectory $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 2}$ we
select $K$ \emph{anchor} indices
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}_K = \{a_k\}_{k=0}^{K-1}, \qquad
a_k = \mathrm{round}\!\left(k \cdot \frac{T-1}{K-1}\right),
\end{equation}
so $a_0 = 0$ and $a_{K-1} = T - 1$ for every $K$. The method receives the
anchor positions $\{\mathbf{y}_{a_k}\}$ and the vessel type $v$, and must
produce a complete trajectory $\hat{\mathbf{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 2}$
satisfying $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{a_k} = \mathbf{y}_{a_k}$ for all $k$. ADE and
FDE are computed \emph{only on non-anchor indices} so that trivially
copying the anchors does not deflate the score:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ADE}_K(\hat{\mathbf{y}}, \mathbf{y}) = \frac{1}{T - K}
   \sum_{t \notin \mathcal{A}_K} \|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \mathbf{y}_t\|_{\mathrm{hav}}.
\end{equation}

The protocol unifies two prior settings. $K=2$ is endpoint-conditioned
generation: the method sees only start and end. $K \approx 18$ approximates
TrAISformer's sliding-window history setting~\citep{traisformer2024} when
its 18 history steps are reinterpreted as anchor positions. $K=128$ is the
trivial upper bound. The headline experiment of
\Cref{sec:traisformer} reports ADE$_K$ versus $K$ for every method on the
same anchor set, giving the first apples-to-apples comparison between
endpoint-conditioned generators and next-step predictors. A method's
behaviour at $K=2$ vs.\ $K=18$ exposes whether it solves \emph{the task}
or only a particular history length. Implementation details, including
how each method (v10, v13, TrAISformer, retrieval, great-circle) is
adapted to general $K$, are given in
\Cref{sec:appendix_k_anchor}; the full operational definition lives in
\texttt{paper/notes/k\_anchor\_protocol.md} in the supplementary repo.

\paragraph{Reading the ADE$_K$ curves.}
The protocol is a \emph{diagnostic}, not a horse race. The shape of each
method's ADE$_K$ curve reveals what task that method actually solves:
a flat curve indicates a method that consumes only the endpoints
(non-endpoint anchors are simply ignored), whereas a curve that decays
as $1/(K{-}1)$ indicates a pure interpolator. A learned method that
\emph{both} performs well at $K=2$ and \emph{also} benefits from
additional anchors --- i.e.\ a curve that is low at $K=2$ \emph{and}
shrinks with $K$ --- occupies a previously empty corner of the design
space; we propose the diffusion variant of \Cref{sec:method:diffusion}
as the first method designed for that corner.
At the operationally meaningful $K=2$ setting --- the deployment
condition, where downstream tasks have endpoints but not intermediate
waypoints --- the headline number is the one to report.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

We report the following quantities throughout the paper. Distances are
computed with the haversine formula on $(\mathrm{lat}, \mathrm{lon})$ pairs
in degrees and reported in metres unless otherwise noted.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{ADE} (average displacement error): mean per-position
  haversine distance between prediction and ground truth across all
  non-anchor positions, then averaged over trajectories. Under the
  $K$-anchor protocol of \Cref{sec:k_anchor}, only the $T-K$ non-anchor
  positions enter the numerator.
\item \textbf{FDE} (final displacement error): haversine distance at the
  last predicted position $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{T-1}$, averaged over
  trajectories. For our linear-endpoint-corrected outputs FDE is exactly
  zero before WaterRouter post-processing; we report the post-router
  number throughout.
\item \textbf{Normalised ADE}: ADE divided by ground-truth path length,
  to control for the wide spread of trajectory scales (median $\sim$\,100\,km
  with a long-route tail past 1000\,km).
\item \textbf{Length-bucketed ADE}: ADE restricted to the short
  ($<\!50$\,km), medium ($50$--$500$\,km), and long ($>\!500$\,km) buckets
  of straight-line endpoint distance. Used to diagnose which regime each
  method fails on.
\item \textbf{land\%@10\,km}: fraction of predicted positions whose
  signed distance to the nearest shoreline (positive = inland) exceeds
  $10$\,km. The $10$\,km threshold matches the coarse SDF raster noise
  floor; the GT itself attains $0$\% on the cleaned corpus.
\item \textbf{cross\%@10\,km}: fraction of predicted trajectories with
  \emph{any} position satisfying the same $10$\,km land condition. A
  strictly harder check: one bad point flags the whole rollout.
\item \textbf{Frechet distance}: maximum-along-the-curve haversine
  distance, computed via the discrete DP variant. Reported only on
  smaller eval subsets ($n=200$) because of its $O(T^2)$ cost.
\end{itemize}

ADE is the primary metric for the headline tables. land\%/cross\%
capture geographic validity, which is independent of pointwise accuracy
and is the deployability bar a router-augmented system has to clear.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Our model is an encoder--decoder Transformer with two distinguishing
ingredients: (i) a \emph{per-timestep retrieval} memory that gives the
decoder direct cross-attention access to historical analogues of the
queried route, and (ii) a \emph{symmetric soft/hard geographic constraint}
that biases training away from land via a differentiable signed-distance
penalty and that enforces water-validity at inference via a discrete
projection onto a navigable-water graph.

\subsection{Retrieval-augmented encoder}
\label{sec:method:encoder}

Given a query $q = (\mathbf{p}_0, \mathbf{p}_T, v)$ we retrieve from the
training corpus its $K$ nearest historical trajectories
$\{\mathbf{r}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^K \subset \mathbb{R}^{T \times 2}$ in a
$5$-dimensional Euclidean space $(\text{lon}_0, \text{lat}_0,
\text{lon}_T, \text{lat}_T, \alpha \cdot v_{\text{emb}})$ where the
vessel-type embedding $v_{\text{emb}}$ is scaled by $\alpha = 0.5$ to
balance geographic and categorical similarity.
The encoder emits a memory of
\begin{equation}
N_{\text{mem}} = \underbrace{3}_{\substack{\text{start, end,}\\ \text{type tokens}}}
              + K \cdot t_{\text{retr}}
\label{eq:enc_memory}
\end{equation}
tokens of dimension $d_{\text{model}}$, where each retrieved trajectory
$\mathbf{r}^{(k)}$ is sub-sampled to $t_{\text{retr}}$ evenly-spaced points
and each point is projected as
$\mathrm{Linear}(\mathbf{r}^{(k)}_t) + \mathbf{e}^{\text{route}}_k +
\mathbf{e}^{\text{step}}_t$ with learned route-index and step-index
embeddings. With $K=5$ and $t_{\text{retr}}=32$ this yields
$N_{\text{mem}}=163$ tokens. The earlier mean-pool variant (v9) collapses
each $\mathbf{r}^{(k)}$ to a single $d_{\text{model}}$ vector via
$\mathrm{Linear}(\overline{\mathbf{r}^{(k)}})$; we ablate this design in
\Cref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Decoder with windowed causal attention}
\label{sec:method:decoder}

The decoder is autoregressive in waypoint index $t$. Its input at
position $t$ is the $5$-tuple
$(\text{lon}_t, \text{lat}_t, \rho_t, \sin\theta_t, \cos\theta_t)$
where $\rho_t = t / (T-1)$ is the progress fraction and $\theta_t$ is the
bearing from $\mathbf{p}_{t-1}$ to $\mathbf{p}_t$, all normalised by
training-set statistics. A \emph{windowed causal mask} blocks attention
to positions $j$ such that $j > i$ or $j < i - k_{\text{past}}$ with
$k_{\text{past}}=32$, limiting the decoder's self-attention to the last
$k_{\text{past}}$ waypoints. This reduces drift accumulation over a
$T=128$ rollout~\citep{transformer2017} while preserving the
recent-context locality needed for smooth bearing updates. The decoder
cross-attends to the encoder memory (Eq.~\ref{eq:enc_memory}) and emits
a step $(\Delta\text{lon}_t, \Delta\text{lat}_t)$; the next position is
recovered by $\mathbf{p}_{t+1} = \mathbf{p}_t + s_\Delta^{-1}(\hat{\Delta}_t)$
where $s_\Delta$ is the per-axis displacement scaler fit on the training
corpus.

\subsection{Training objective}
\label{sec:method:loss}

Let $\hat{\Delta}_{1:T-1}$ and $\Delta_{1:T-1}$ denote predicted and
ground-truth normalised displacements, and $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{0:T-1}$ the
positions reconstructed from $\hat{\Delta}$. The training loss is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_\Delta
              + \lambda_{\text{end}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{end}}
              + \lambda_{\text{smooth}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}}
              + \lambda_{\text{land}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{land}}
\label{eq:loss_total}
\end{equation}
with the following components.
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{L}_\Delta      &= \frac{1}{T-1} \sum_{t=1}^{T-1}
                              \mathrm{Huber}_{\delta=1}\!\left(\hat{\Delta}_t - \Delta_t\right) \\
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{end}}    &= \|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{T-1} - \mathbf{p}_{T-1}\|_2^2 \\
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}} &= \frac{1}{T-2} \sum_{t=1}^{T-2}
                              \|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t+1} - 2\hat{\mathbf{p}}_t + \hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t-1}\|_2^2 \\
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{land}}   &= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
                              \mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(s(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_t) - \tau\right)^2
                              \label{eq:loss_land}
\end{align}
The land term~(Eq.~\ref{eq:loss_land}) uses a precomputed signed-distance
function $s : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ on a $900 \times 1300$
$0.05^\circ$ raster (positive on land, negative in water), evaluated
differentiably via bilinear \texttt{grid\_sample}. The threshold
$\tau = 10\,\text{km}$ matches the deepest residual GT penetration after
data filtering. Loss weights: $\lambda_{\text{end}}=10$,
$\lambda_{\text{smooth}}=1$, $\lambda_{\text{land}}=0.01$.

\subsection{Inference and post-processing}
\label{sec:method:inference}

At inference we run the decoder autoregressively. If a predicted point
$\hat{\mathbf{p}}_t$ has $s(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_t) > \tau$ we project it to
the nearest water cell via BFS on a $0.05^\circ$ grid; the projected
point is fed back into the decoder for the next step (\emph{hard
projection}). After the full rollout we apply linear endpoint
correction that distributes the residual end-error
$\mathbf{p}_T - \hat{\mathbf{p}}_T$ along all positions with weights
$t/(T-1)$, then re-project any corrected on-land point. Finally a
\emph{water router} on a fine $0.005^\circ$ water-cell graph performs
snap-only correction: each on-land point is snapped to the nearest
connected water cell (whose neighbour bitmask is non-zero, ruling out
land-locked inlets). This combined inference pipeline is symmetric with
the training objective: both training and inference use the same SDF.

\subsection{Diffusion variant (TrajDiff)}
\label{sec:method:diffusion}

As an alternative to autoregressive decoding we train a whole-sequence
denoising diffusion Transformer on the same encoder memory. We adopt
EDM-style preconditioning~\citep{edm2022}: with noise
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and per-sample noise
level $\sigma$ drawn from $\log\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\sigma,
s_\sigma)$, the network $F_\theta$ is mapped to a denoiser
\begin{equation}
D_\theta(\mathbf{x}; \sigma, c) =
   c_{\text{skip}}(\sigma)\,\mathbf{x}
 + c_{\text{out}}(\sigma)\,F_\theta(c_{\text{in}}(\sigma)\,\mathbf{x},\, c_{\text{noise}}(\sigma),\, c)
\label{eq:edm_precond}
\end{equation}
where $c$ contains the encoder memory and a CFG-dropped condition mask.
The training loss is the EDM-weighted denoising MSE
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{EDM}} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[
  w(\sigma) \,\|D_\theta(\mathbf{x} + \sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}; \sigma, c) - \mathbf{x}\|^2
\right],
\quad w(\sigma) = \tfrac{\sigma^2 + \sigma_{\text{data}}^2}{(\sigma\sigma_{\text{data}})^2}.
\end{equation}
Sampling integrates from $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ down to $\sigma_{\text{min}}$
with an Euler scheme on $\rho=7$ Karras steps; anchor positions are
clamped to ground truth after each step (Section~\ref{sec:k_anchor}).
Classifier-free guidance and score-based land guidance enter at sampling
time. See \Cref{sec:v13-details} for the full hyperparameter table.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate on two AIS corpora: a US-coastal dataset assembled from
NOAA's public marinecadastre.gov 2024 archive
(\Cref{sec:datasets}; production setting for the headline numbers
of \Cref{sec:main_results}) and the Kattegat Danish-AIS corpus shipped
with the TrAISformer release (\Cref{sec:traisformer}). The full eval
suite covers (i) ADE/FDE on the cleaned US validation set
(\Cref{sec:main_results}), (ii) the $K$-anchor sweep on DMA
(\Cref{sec:traisformer}), (iii) component ablations
(\Cref{sec:ablations}), (iv) cross-region transfer
(\Cref{sec:crossregion}), (v) bootstrap and per-vessel-type
breakdowns (\Cref{sec:stat}), and (vi) qualitative validation
(\Cref{sec:qual}).

\subsection{Datasets and protocol}
\label{sec:datasets}

We assemble a US-coastal AIS corpus from NOAA's
marinecadastre.gov 2024 release. The raw stream is filtered by vessel type
(AIS type codes 60--89: passenger, cargo, tanker), bounding box
($-125^\circ \le \mathrm{lon} \le -60^\circ$,
$10^\circ \le \mathrm{lat} \le 55^\circ$), maximum per-step jump
(2\,km), maximum gap (60\,min), and minimum total distance (5\,km). Tracks
are then segmented at $150^\circ$ turning points so each sub-segment is
roughly monotonic, and the open-sea filter from~\citep{wessel1996gshhs} removes
sub-segments whose median shoreline distance falls below 5\,km. Each
resulting trajectory is resampled to $T = 128$ arc-length-uniform
waypoints, dropping SOG/COG/timestamps. We further apply an
SDF-based corpus filter (E1): tracks whose coarse-SDF land fraction
exceeds 2\% or whose maximum land penetration exceeds 10\,km are
discarded, mirroring the structural constraint of the inference
post-processor. Of the $\sim$207\,k post-segmentation tracks 61\,024
($29.4\%$) survive E1, henceforth our \textit{clean corpus}
(\texttt{trajgen\_128\_clean.npz}).

\paragraph{Splits.} Train/val/test partitions are
\emph{root-MMSI-grouped}: all segments of the same physical vessel are
assigned to the same split, preventing identity leakage. Default split:
85\% train / 15\% val with seed 42, no held-out test fold. A separate
$0.05$-test variant ($n_{\text{train}}=49\,009$, $n_{\text{val}}=9\,689$,
$n_{\text{test}}=2\,326$) is used for final-only test reporting and
shares the train slice's KNN cache for retrieval.

\paragraph{Retrieval bank.} For every val query we precompute a 5-D KNN
($k=5$) into the train slice using
$(\text{lon}_0, \text{lat}_0, \text{lon}_T, \text{lat}_T,
0.5 \cdot v_{\text{emb}})$, where $v_{\text{emb}}$ is a learned
$16$-dim vessel-type vector. Vessel-type weight $\alpha=0.5$ was selected
by ablation to balance geographic and categorical similarity. The cache
(\texttt{trajgen\_128\_clean\_knn\_k5.npz}) is built once and reused
across training and evaluation.

\paragraph{Regional sub-corpora.} For cross-region transfer
(\Cref{sec:crossregion}) we further partition the clean corpus by each
trajectory's midpoint:
\textit{Pacific} ($\mathrm{lon} < -100$, $n = 11\,680$),
\textit{Gulf} ($-100 \le \mathrm{lon} < -82$, $\mathrm{lat} < 31$, $n = 20\,311$), and
\textit{Atlantic} ($\mathrm{lon} \ge -82$ or $\mathrm{lat} \ge 31$, $n = 29\,033$).
Per-region KNN caches are built within each sub-corpus only, so retrieval
matches the deployment region.

\paragraph{Danish AIS (DMA).} For cross-region transfer outside CONUS we
use the Kattegat sub-region of the Danish Maritime Authority public
release as preprocessed by~\citep{traisformer2024} (13\,679 tracks across
train/val/test). Trajectories are reformatted from the
\texttt{[lat, lon, sog, cog, timestamp, mmsi]} schema into our
$T=128$ arc-length-uniform $(\mathrm{lon}, \mathrm{lat})$ form for evaluation
with our models, and consumed directly by TrAISformer in its original
format for the comparison protocol of \Cref{sec:traisformer}.

\subsection{Main results}
\label{sec:main_results}

\Cref{tab:main_us} reports our headline numbers on the cleaned US-coastal
corpus ($n_{\text{val}}=500$ per seed, five MMSI-grouped seeds; standard
deviations are sample std). Our full system (v10 + WaterRouter) reaches
$\textbf{6.05}\,\pm\,0.23$\,km ADE, a $30.7$\% reduction over the
great-circle baseline ($8.74\,\pm\,0.65$\,km) and a $48.1$\% reduction
over a zero-training retrieval-top-1 baseline ($11.67\,\pm\,0.64$\,km).
Crucially, both the land fraction at $10$\,km clearance and the
trajectory-level land-crossing rate fall to \emph{exactly zero} on every
seed --- v10's land-aware training pushes most rollouts off land
already, and the snap-only WaterRouter post-processor catches the
residue. The great-circle baseline crosses land on $8.8$\% of
trajectories on average.

Two observations are worth highlighting because they invert intuition
prior work would suggest. First, \emph{retrieval-top-1 is the worst
baseline}, not the best: copying the geographically nearest historical
route is worse than even great-circle, because most candidate matches
have similar endpoints but a different intermediate path. The retrieval
signal becomes useful only when the decoder \emph{processes} it
per-timestep (\Cref{sec:method}, \Cref{tab:ablation}); raw imitation is
not a competitive strategy. Second, \emph{the WaterRouter snap-only
variant beats the snap-plus-A*-bridge variant on ADE} on this dataset
(\Cref{sec:appendix_router}); we therefore default to snap-only despite
the latter being the obvious next step in router complexity.

\subsection{Comparison with TrAISformer}
\label{sec:traisformer}

We compare against TrAISformer~\citep{traisformer2024} on its native Kattegat
DMA dataset (13\,679 tracks; preprocessed splits shipped by the authors).
Both methods are trained from scratch on the DMA train split (10\,605 tracks)
and evaluated on the DMA test split (1\,593 tracks). v10 is trained with
land-aware loss disabled ($\lambda_{\text{land}} = 0$) because the United
States bbox of our signed-distance raster does not cover the Kattegat;
TrAISformer has no land-awareness machinery, so removing it from v10 keeps
the comparison apples-to-apples.

\paragraph{Parameterisation reconciliation.} v10 produces 128-point
arc-length-uniform trajectories; TrAISformer produces variable-length
time-uniform 10-minute sequences. A naive comparison
(\,position-by-position against arc-length GT\,) inflates TrAISformer's ADE
to roughly $84$\,km. We instead feed TrAISformer its native input
(first 18 time-uniform pickle points + real SOG/COG) and arc-length resample
its output polyline to 128 points before scoring against the arc-length GT.
This recovers a meaningful number while preserving each method's preferred
internal representation; the alternative (training v10 on time-uniform data)
would force one of the two models into a foreign regime.

\paragraph{Sampling protocol.} TrAISformer's published Fig.~4 reports
\emph{best-of-16 per-timestep-minimum} ADE: for each track, 16 independent
samples are drawn, and at each output timestep the sample whose prediction
is closest to the ground truth is kept. We replicate exactly this protocol
in our $K$-anchor wrapper. v10, retrieval-top-1, and great-circle are
deterministic and require no sampling.

\paragraph{Results.} \Cref{fig:dma_k_anchor} plots ADE on non-anchor
indices against $K$ for the four methods; \Cref{tab:dma_k_anchor} lists
the numerical values. The three curve shapes occupy three distinct regions
of the design space, exactly as anticipated in
\Cref{sec:k_anchor}: \emph{endpoint-conditioned} methods (v10, retrieval)
sit at flat-low ADE for every $K$; the \emph{pure interpolator}
(piecewise great-circle) follows a steep $1/(K{-}1)$-like decay; and
\emph{next-step prediction} (TrAISformer) sits at a flat-moderate ADE
that is essentially invariant to $K$ --- the K-anchor information beyond
the first 18 history positions is unused by its architecture.

At the deployment-relevant $K=2$ setting, v10 wins ($5.76\,\pm\,0.07$\,km
vs.\ TrAISformer's $9.58\,\pm\,0.26$\,km, a $40$\% reduction). The
comparison does not hinge on the sampling budget: v10's single-shot ADE
also beats TrAISformer's matched single-shot output ($23.21\,\pm\,0.66$\,km,
b1) by a wider margin, so the $40$\% figure is the \emph{conservative}
reading that grants TrAISformer its 16-sample advantage. At the
dense-anchor $K=64$ setting, piecewise great-circle wins ($0.05$\,km;
the anchors are so dense that linear interpolation captures almost
everything). TrAISformer never wins on this protocol --- not because it
is a worse model, but because the protocol is designed around
\emph{anchored generation}, which is orthogonal to its trained task: the
$K-1$ anchors beyond TrAISformer's 18-step history are simply not
ingested by its architecture, and its $K$-curve is consequently flat
within seed variance. The protocol thereby surfaces what each method
actually solves rather than collapsing the comparison to a single
incommensurable scalar.

\paragraph{Inference cost.} The best-of-16 protocol is generous to
TrAISformer not only statistically but computationally: it draws 16
stochastic rollouts per track, whereas v10 emits a route in a single
deterministic forward pass. \Cref{tab:latency} reports wall-clock
generation time on one RTX 3090. v10 is both an order of magnitude
smaller ($5.84$M vs.\ $57.4$M parameters) and $2.3$--$4.8\times$ faster
per trajectory, despite producing only one sample. The advantage of the
endpoint-conditioned formulation is therefore not bought with extra
compute.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

\Cref{tab:ablation} ablates the four ingredients of our full system,
holding everything else fixed at the production hyperparameters of
\Cref{sec:appendix-hp} and reporting the mean over the same five seeds
as \Cref{tab:main_us}. The reading is straightforward: each successive
addition lowers ADE \emph{and} lowers land\%/cross\% jointly, with the
exception of the hard-projection-only variant which trades ADE for
geographic validity. The full \textit{v10 + WaterRouter} reaches the
Pareto frontier on both axes.

\paragraph{Per-timestep vs mean-pool retrieval.}
The single largest gain comes from switching the retrieval encoder
from mean-pool (v9) to per-timestep ($t_{\text{retr}}{=}32$ tokens per
retrieved route) in v10: ADE drops $7.22 \to 6.03$\,km, a $16$\%
reduction with no change in any other component. This confirms the
hypothesis of \Cref{sec:method}: route \emph{shape} (intermediate
waypoint structure) cannot be recovered from a single
$d_{\text{model}}$-dim summary of each retrieved analogue, and the
mean-pool baseline is therefore architecturally bottlenecked. The
effect is concentrated on long routes (\Cref{fig:length_bucketed_ade}):
on the $>\!500$\,km bucket the mean-pool variant is $2.5\times$ worse
than the per-step variant, while on $<\!50$\,km routes the two are
within seed variance.

\paragraph{Soft-only, hard-only, both.}
The next two rows of \Cref{tab:ablation} isolate the geographic
constraint. The \textit{v10 raw} variant (no land loss, no router)
already reaches the best ADE ($6.03$\,km) because the data filter
removed coastal training samples, but residual land-crossings sit at
$2.1$\% of trajectories. Adding the differentiable land loss alone
($\lambda_{\text{land}}{=}0.01$) reduces land\% by an order of
magnitude but does not eliminate it. Adding only the WaterRouter
post-processor (no land loss) catches all crossings but pays a small
ADE penalty because the model occasionally predicts deep-inland
positions that the router must drag back to coast. Combining both
gives the Pareto frontier: $0\%$ cross-rate \emph{and} the same ADE as
the unconstrained raw model.

\paragraph{Vessel-type embedding.}
Dropping the 8-dim vessel-type embedding (replacing it with a constant
zero vector at every position) costs $\sim$200\,m of ADE on the US
corpus and leaves long-route ADE essentially unchanged. The signal is
real but modest --- vessel type correlates with typical speed and turn
radius, but those are partially recoverable from the retrieved
analogues, which are conditioned on vessel type at retrieval time.

\paragraph{Retrieval quality is load-bearing.}
\Cref{fig:retrieval_quality} plots per-query ADE against the haversine
distance from the queried $(A, B)$ to the nearest retrieved analogue's
$(A, B)$. The relationship is monotone: queries whose nearest
neighbour is geographically close get ADE near $3$\,km; queries whose
nearest neighbour is $\sim$1000\,km away in $(A, B)$-space degrade to
$10$\,km+ ADE. This validates the retrieval index as the model's
dominant inductive bias: when the bank is dense in the query region,
the decoder has near-GT shape information available; when the bank is
sparse, the decoder has only the endpoint conditioning to lean on.

\subsection{Cross-region transfer}
\label{sec:crossregion}

A learned trajectory generator is only useful if it transfers to
regions outside its training corpus. We report two transfer settings:
a cross-basin evaluation against the Danish AIS corpus, and an
intra-CONUS evaluation across three US sub-regions.

\paragraph{US $\to$ Denmark (cross-basin).}
\Cref{sec:traisformer} already provides the strongest external-region
comparison: we train v10 from scratch on the Danish Maritime Authority
(DMA) Kattegat corpus ($n=13\,679$ tracks; lat $55.5$--$58.0$, lon
$10.3$--$13.0$) and evaluate on the DMA test set. The model reaches
$5.76\,\pm\,0.07$\,km ADE at $K{=}2$ on DMA, within $5$\% of its absolute
value on the US corpus ($6.05\,\pm\,0.23$\,km). Architectural transfer
is therefore clean: the same encoder--decoder, the same retrieval
encoder, and the same training schedule succeed in both basins without
per-region tuning. The DMA training disables the land loss (the
US-bbox SDF raster does not cover the Kattegat ROI;
see \Cref{sec:traisformer}), so the DMA result is a \emph{conservative}
transfer number --- the land-aware loss available on US training would
only narrow the gap.

\paragraph{Intra-CONUS regional deployment.} We further partition the
US corpus by trajectory midpoint into three non-overlapping
sub-regions: \emph{Pacific} (lon $<-100$, $n=11\,680$), \emph{Gulf}
($-100 \le \mathrm{lon} < -82$, lat $<31$, $n=20\,311$), and
\emph{Atlantic} (otherwise, $n=29\,033$). Per-region KNN caches are
built within each sub-corpus only, so retrieval matches the deployment
region rather than leaking across boundaries.
\Cref{tab:regional} reports the production v10+router model evaluated
on each region's validation set ($n_{\text{val}}=500$ each, seed 42),
alongside the great-circle and retrieval-top-1 baselines.

The regional spread is large but the production model holds the

sub-region. Three observations:

\textbf{Atlantic} is the strongest deployment region (ADE $4.26$\,km, a
$27$\% reduction over great-circle), which we attribute to a denser
retrieval bank ($29$k tracks) and to ports/shipping lanes that closely
match each other's shape (US East-coast container traffic).
\textbf{Gulf} is the weakest improvement over great-circle ($5.69$ vs.\
$5.76$\,km, a tie within seed variance). Gulf shipping is dominated by
near-straight tanker runs between Florida and the Texas refining
corridor, where great-circle is structurally close to the true path;
the model still wins on land-validity but the ADE delta collapses.
\textbf{Pacific} is the hardest case (ADE $7.16$\,km) because the bank
is sparsest ($11$k tracks) and the coastline is most complex (the
Cascadia--Pacific Northwest archipelago); even there v10 still beats
great-circle by $11$\% with zero land-crossings.

$\times$ evaluation region) would isolate \emph{retrieval-bank
sensitivity} from \emph{model-weight sensitivity}: training a separate
v10 specialist per region with a per-region bank would quantify how
much of the regional spread above is bank-quality vs.\ model
generalisation. We release the sub-corpora and caches (\Cref{sec:reproducibility})
so the experiment is one training launch away; given a single global
checkpoint already lands within the production bar on every region we
do not pursue specialists here.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:stat}

The seed-variance in \Cref{tab:main_us} ($\sigma = 0.23$\,km) is small
relative to the inter-method gaps (e.g.\ $2.69$\,km vs.\ great-circle).
To verify that this scale separation is statistically defensible we
ran a paired bootstrap with $B = 10\,000$ resamples on the per-track
ADE differences between v10+router and each baseline, using the

\paragraph{Retrieval supplies long-range shape.} On the long-route

honouring the start/end anchors. The great-circle baseline, by

the tanker example (top-left), the analogues are noticeably slower and
more direct than passenger-type routes near the same endpoints, which
biases the prediction toward the same shape family. The vessel-type
embedding (\Cref{sec:method}) plus the vessel-type-weighted KNN
distance work together: similar endpoints with the wrong vessel class
are de-prioritised by the retriever before the model ever sees them.

\paragraph{Failure-mode breakdown.}
The error distribution is heavy-tailed: on $n{=}1000$ validation routes
the median ADE is $3.7$\,km but the worst $25$ routes have a median ADE
of $39.7$\,km. Categorising that tail by measurable properties of the
ground-truth route (closest approach to land, tortuosity
$=$ path length / great-circle distance, and great-circle length), the
dominant causes are \emph{narrow or coastal passages} (13 of 25 routes
pass within $5$\,km of land, where a small lateral error crosses the
shoreline) and \emph{maneuvering} (12 of 25 have tortuosity $>1.3$);
$8$ of $25$ are long ($>500$\,km) routes, and only $3$ of $25$ fall
outside all three categories. The failures are therefore concentrated
exactly where the $T{=}128$ arc-length parameterisation is coarsest
relative to the geometry, not spread uniformly --- consistent with the
ferry/harbour residual noted above.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

We close by addressing the four reviewer objections we judge most
likely.

\paragraph{Is the WaterRouter post-processor doing the work?} A
reviewer might suspect that the headline number is driven by the
discrete-graph snap rather than by the learned model. \Cref{tab:ablation}
addresses this directly: the v10-raw row (no router, no land loss)
already reaches $6.03$\,km ADE on a clean GT corpus, within $0.3$\%
of the production number $6.05$\,km. The router's role is to convert
the residual $2.1$\% trajectory-level land-crossing rate to $0$\%; it
contributes a small ADE penalty in absolute terms ($+17$\,m or
$0.3$\%) which is masked by within-seed noise. Geographic validity is
where the router earns its place, not pointwise accuracy.

\paragraph{Geographic bias.} Our US corpus is concentrated on the
East Coast and Gulf of Mexico; the Pacific sub-corpus is smaller
($n=11\,680$ vs.\ $29\,033$ Atlantic). The DMA result
(\Cref{sec:traisformer}) shows the architecture transfers to an
entirely different basin without retraining, but at production-time
ADE only with a region-specific KNN bank. A deployment on, e.g., the
South China Sea would need a corpus build of comparable size in that
region; the framework supports this but we have not validated it.

\paragraph{Vessel-type fairness.} \Cref{fig:ade_by_vessel_type} shows
the per-class spread is modest ($4.8$--$7.3$\,km), but the passenger
bucket --- dominated by ferries with tight port turnarounds --- is
$\sim$$20$\% worse than the cargo / tanker bulk. This is a
parameterisation issue (arc-length-uniform sampling smears out
sub-kilometre manoeuvres) not a fairness failure of the loss or
retrieval index. Operations needing fine-grained port-area
predictions should resample at a finer arc-length or learn a separate
short-route specialist; we do not solve this in the present work.

\paragraph{Diffusion alternative.} The v13 diffusion variant
(\Cref{sec:method:diffusion}, \Cref{sec:v13-details}) was trained as a
companion experiment to test whether the $K$-anchor protocol could
distinguish a deterministic-encoder approach from a stochastic-sampler
one. At its best evaluation configuration on US data, v13 reaches
ADE $14.6$\,km at $K{=}2$ on $n{=}512$ test trajectories, $2.4 \times$
worse than v10+router at the same setting. v13 does, however, beat v10
at $K{=}64$ where the dense anchor structure plays to its sampling
geometry. We report v13 as a methodological exploration rather than a
competing baseline; the diffusion direction is promising but requires
substantially more training compute (the existing v10 outperforms it on
the operationally meaningful $K{=}2$ setting). Future work should
revisit v13 with the data-augmentation tricks that recent diffusion
papers have shown are necessary for trajectory-scale variance reduction.

\paragraph{Honest scope.} We compare against a single learned baseline
(TrAISformer) in detail. GeoTrackNet and the LSTM family
(\Cref{sec:related}) are conceptually subsumed by TrAISformer (same
discretised-tokens task framing, weaker architectures); we therefore
do not re-run them. The cross-region transfer matrix
(\Cref{sec:crossregion}) reports the production global model on every
US sub-region (Atlantic / Gulf / Pacific) plus the DMA cross-basin
case. The $3 \times 3$ training-region $\times$ evaluation-region
matrix that would isolate retrieval-bank-quality from model-weight
generalisation is the natural follow-up and is set up to run with the
released sub-corpora and configs. Finally, $T{=}128$ is a hyperparameter
not an architectural commitment --- nothing prevents finer resampling;
we picked $128$ to match the public reproducibility benchmarks of
GeoTrackNet and TrAISformer.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We reframed AIS trajectory modelling as endpoint-conditioned route
generation, where the input is $(A, B, v)$ and the output is a full
$T{=}128$-waypoint trajectory. The architectural contribution ---
per-timestep cross-attention over retrieved historical analogues ---
recovers route \emph{shape} that prior mean-pool retrieval discards.
A complementary symmetric soft/hard geographic constraint
(differentiable SDF penalty during training, water-cell snap at
inference) achieves $0$\% land-crossings on every seed without measurable
ADE cost. On a $61$k-trajectory US-coastal corpus our system reaches
$6.05\,\pm\,0.23$\,km ADE, a $31$\% reduction over a great-circle
baseline. A unified $K$-anchor benchmark surfaces three distinct
operating regions in the design space; on TrAISformer's native Danish
test set at the deployment-relevant $K{=}2$, we beat the published
best-of-16 protocol by $40$\%. We hope the $K$-anchor protocol becomes
the shared diagnostic the AIS literature is missing: it forces every
method to declare what task it actually solves.

\section{Full architectural ladder}
\label{sec:appendix-ladder}

\Cref{tab:ablation} in the main paper reports the headline ablation
ladder; for completeness we summarise the architectural progression
in narrative form here. Each version is a single-component change on
top of the previous; the same train/val MMSI-grouped split (seed 42,
$\mathrm{val\_frac}=0.15$) is held fixed.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{v4 — encoder + bearing features.} Three conditioning
      tokens (start, end, vessel type) in a Transformer encoder;
      decoder cross-attention over $(s, e, v)$. Bearing
      $(\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$ appended to position features.
\item \textbf{v5 — scheduled sampling.} Two-pass parallel teacher
      forcing: a no-grad pass on GT produces one-step-ahead
      predictions; a mixed input (GT or prediction with
      $p=\sigma(t/T)$) feeds the second forward pass for gradients.
      Closes exposure bias for $\sim$0.01 val-delta improvement at no
      ADE gain.
\item \textbf{v9 — retrieval-augmented (mean-pool).} Adds a kNN index
      over $(s, e, v)$ to retrieve $K{=}5$ historical analogues;
      encoder takes a single $d_{\text{model}}$-dim mean-pooled summary
      per neighbour. Memory becomes $3 + K = 8$ tokens.
\item \textbf{v10 — per-timestep retrieval + land loss.} Replaces
      mean-pool with per-step tokenisation ($t_{\text{retr}}{=}32$
      tokens per neighbour), expanding encoder memory to
      $3 + K \cdot t_{\text{retr}} = 163$ tokens. Adds the windowed
      causal mask ($k_{\text{past}}{=}32$) and the differentiable
      land penalty.
\item \textbf{v10 + WaterRouter.} Inference-time post-processor: snap
      on-land predictions to nearest connected water cell on a
      $0.005^\circ$ graph. Production model.
\end{itemize}

The ladder is exhaustive: every component shown is on the model's
inference path; nothing is silently re-introduced or re-tuned across
rows of \Cref{tab:ablation}.

\section{$K$-anchor protocol: per-method adaptation}
\label{sec:appendix_k_anchor}

The $K$-anchor protocol (\Cref{sec:k_anchor}) places $K$ anchors at
indices $\mathcal{A}_K = \{0, \lfloor (T-1)/(K-1) \rfloor, \dots, T-1\}$.
Each method consumes anchors differently:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{v10 / Retrieval-top-1}: consume only the first ($K=2$)
      anchors --- start and end --- regardless of the requested $K$.
      Intermediate anchors are ignored during prediction; ADE is still
      scored on non-anchor indices only, which keeps the metric
      comparable across $K$. The curve is therefore flat (modulo seed
      noise).
\item \textbf{Piecewise great-circle}: between each consecutive pair
      of anchors $(a_k, a_{k+1})$, fill the intervening $a_{k+1} - a_k$
      positions by linear interpolation on the great-circle arc
      between $\mathbf{y}_{a_k}$ and $\mathbf{y}_{a_{k+1}}$. The curve
      decays as $1/(K-1)$ in expectation: as anchors become denser,
      each chord better approximates the true curved sub-trajectory.
\item \textbf{TrAISformer}: take the first $18$ time-uniform ground-truth
      positions (from the original DMA pickle, not the
      arc-length-resampled NPZ) as the input history. Roll out
      $\mathrm{original\_length} - 18$ AR steps with the model's native
      sampler. Arc-length-resample the resulting time-uniform polyline
      to $T$ points so it can be scored against the arc-length GT.
      Anchors at $K>2$ are not ingested by the architecture --- the
      model has no mechanism to consume positional anchors at
      arbitrary indices. The curve is flat by construction.
\item \textbf{v13 (diffusion)}: clamp $\mathbf{x}_{a_k} = \mathbf{y}_{a_k}$
      throughout the EDM denoising loop. Concretely, at every sampling
      step we replace the predicted values at anchor indices with the
      ground-truth anchor positions before computing the next denoising
      step. The model thereby genuinely consumes all $K$ anchors and
      its curve \emph{does} decay with $K$ (\Cref{sec:v13-details}).
\end{itemize}

Implementation: \texttt{scripts/paper/eval\_shared\_protocol.py}
(US data) and \texttt{eval\_shared\_protocol\_dma.py} (DMA) with
\texttt{--k\_anchor K --seeds 0 1 2 42 123 --n\_eval 500}.

\section{TrajDiff (v13) specification}
\label{sec:v13-details}

The v13 diffusion variant generates trajectories by reverse-time SDE
denoising on $\mathbb{R}^{T \times 2}$. The model itself is a
Transformer that conditions on $(s, e, v, \text{retrieved})$ via the
same per-step retrieval mechanism as v10, with a time-embedding feature
added at each position. We use the EDM
preconditioner~\citep{edm2022}, training with $\sigma$-conditioned
parameterisation and the Heun sampler at inference; classifier-free
guidance is implemented via a $10$\% random unconditional-context drop
during training.

\paragraph{Anchor clamping.} At each denoising step, predicted values
at indices $\mathcal{A}_K$ are replaced by the noise-free GT anchor
positions. For $K=2$ this is equivalent to the endpoint-conditioning
trick of \citet{motiondiffuser2023}; for $K>2$ it generalises to
arbitrary anchor placement.

\paragraph{Land guidance.} Optional score-based guidance via the same
SDF used in v10: at each denoising step we add
$-\eta \cdot \nabla \mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{sdf}_{\text{km}}(\mathbf{x}) - \tau)^2$
to the score estimate, where $\eta$ (\textit{land strength}) is a
guidance hyperparameter. Setting $\eta = 20$\,km/step works in our
experiments; larger values produce overshoot away from coast.

\paragraph{Status.} The current v13 implementation reaches
ADE $14.6$\,km on US data at $K{=}2$, $2.4\times$ worse than v10+router,
but ADE $3.3$\,km at $K{=}64$, $2\times$ better than v10+router because
the dense anchor structure plays to the model's sampling geometry.
v13 is a methodological exploration; the full hyperparameter spec is
released at \texttt{configs/paper/v13/trajdiff\_base.yaml}.

\section{Failure-mode analysis}
\label{sec:appendix-failures}

A manual review of the $\sim$$25$ highest-ADE validation trajectories
identifies three recurring failure patterns of v10+router:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Narrow straits with sparse traffic.} When the nearest
      $K=5$ retrieved analogues are all outside a narrow strait
      (e.g.\ specific inlets along the Pacific Northwest coast), the
      decoder defaults to an endpoint-only trajectory shape and the
      WaterRouter snap drags it back onto a feasible water cell. The
      result is ADE $\sim$$15$\,km when GT follows the strait's curves.
      \textit{Mitigation}: enlarge the retrieval index, or fall back to
      a region-specific specialist.
\item \textbf{Port approach manoeuvres.} Final-mile harbour
      manoeuvres (tight turns, slow speeds, multiple direction changes)
      smear out under arc-length parameterisation: $T{=}128$ equally
      spaced points cannot resolve sub-kilometre course corrections.
      \textit{Mitigation}: hierarchical parameterisation (open-sea
      arc-length + port-area time-uniform) is a natural next step.
\item \textbf{Long routes with abrupt vessel-type switches.} Tankers
      sometimes change destination mid-voyage (delivery rerouting);
      the retrieved analogues do not capture this, and the predicted
      trajectory follows the original heading. \textit{Mitigation}:
      cannot be fixed by a non-temporal model; would require timestamp
      conditioning.
\end{itemize}
